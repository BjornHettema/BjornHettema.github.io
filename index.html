Ho Chi Minh City, 2025

Bins that tell the truck when they're full

Vollebak puts fill-level sensors inside public waste bins. I built the program that turns those readings into a collection route, so trucks stop driving past empty ones.

A fill level is not a decision. Each reading runs through a logistic curve to give the bin a collection priority, and that priority becomes a time window: a nearly full bin can be collected at any point in the day, a half empty one gets a narrow slot later on.

The whole thing goes to a route optimiser as a set of shipments with windows, costs and service times.

Semarang, Jakarta and Surabaya, 2026

Four hundred year old canals, sinking cities

Java's colonial-era canals still carry the water, but the ground is dropping and the sea is rising. Over five months I worked out which stretches can still be saved.

I built a scoring instrument for individual canal segments and applied it identically in all three cities, so the cases could be compared rather than just described. It ran alongside historical document analysis, QGIS overlays of the original network against the current one, and photographic validation on site.

Sole author, named lead researcher by Universitas Diponegoro, graded 4.0. The study sits inside the Dutch City Deal "Timeless Canals" and runs in both directions, so what Java teaches goes back to the Dutch partners.

Deventer, 2026

A city measured at walking pace

Goudappel asked what a genuinely good pedestrian network for Deventer would look like. I wrote the queries that pulled the base network out of OpenStreetMap and filtered it against explicit rules: alleys, cut-throughs, bridges and tunnels in, private land out. Then we went out and counted whether people used it that way.

Every walkable connection pulled from OpenStreetMap and filtered by hand. The starting point, before the network is classified.

Intensities came from Goudappel's mobility panel, checked against our own counts at three locations. The two datasets did not agree, and what we concluded was that the hard numbers only make sense with street-level observation beside them.

The engineering came first

I spent three years on electrical engineering and technical informatics before switching into spatial development. At the time it felt like starting over. It turned out to be the most useful thing I did, because the problems I care about sit exactly between the two.

A routing algorithm and a zoning framework are both readable to me. That is a smaller club than it should be, and it means I can take a project from the policy question through to the thing that runs.

Fifteen months of that has been fieldwork in Southeast Asia, and it changed how I work. You cannot assess a canal from a desk in Deventer.
